These works deal with epic scope, allegorical or visionary journeys, the human soul, sin and redemption, temptation, divine order, free will, and the interplay of Christian theology with classical and folk traditions. They are frequently discussed alongside Dante's Divine Comedy, Milton's Paradise Lost, Bunyan's Pilgrim's Progress, and Goethe's Faust.

Section I

Foundational Epics & Visionary Journeys

Heroic quests, fate, gods, and human limits—the bedrock of the Western epic tradition.

Greek Epic

Iliad & Odyssey

Homer

The bedrock for later epics including Virgil and Milton. Heroic quests, fate, gods, and human limits.

Roman Epic

Aeneid

Virgil

A pious hero’s journey founding Rome. Hugely influential on Dante (Virgil is his guide) and Milton.

Medieval Epic

The Divine Comedy

Dante Alighieri

The gold standard for moral cosmology and the pilgrimage through Hell, Purgatory, and Paradise.

English Epic

Paradise Lost

John Milton

Cosmic rebellion, the Fall, and redemption. Read with our dedicated companion.

Section II

Allegories of the Christian Life

Everyman journeys, moral struggle, and the path from destruction to redemption.

Christian Allegory

The Pilgrim’s Progress

John Bunyan

The influential everyman’s journey from the City of Destruction to the Celestial City.

Christian Allegory

Grace Abounding to the Chief of Sinners

John Bunyan

A more personal spiritual autobiography from the author of Pilgrim’s Progress.

Philosophical Dialogue

Philosophical dialogue on fortune, evil, and divine providence. The medieval bridge to later works.

Modern Allegory

The Great Divorce / The Screwtape Letters

Modern allegories of heaven and hell, temptation, and choice. Echoes all the above.

Section III

Faustian & Temptation Narratives

The soul-selling scholar, the wager with the devil, and the problem of evil.

Elizabethan Tragedy

Doctor Faustus

Christopher Marlowe

The tragic Elizabethan version of the soul-selling scholar. Pre-Goethe and essential context.

German Epic

Faust (Parts I & II)

Johann Wolfgang von Goethe

Striving, the wager with Mephistopheles, and redemptive humanism. A lifetime’s work.

Russian Novel

Satanic visit to Soviet Moscow—witty, philosophical, and ultimately redemptive.

Russian Novel

Deep dives into faith, doubt, rebellion, and evil. Especially “The Grand Inquisitor.”

Guide

Tips for Reading

Start with Strong Translations

Mandelbaum or Hollander for Dante; modern annotated editions of Milton; Kaufmann for Goethe’s Faust. A good translation makes all the difference.

Use Guided Reading

Notes, audiobooks, and companions reward the density and allusions. The Paradise Lost Experience is designed for exactly this purpose.

Read in Order

Each work builds on what came before. Homer before Virgil, Virgil before Dante, Dante before Milton. Track the allusions as they accumulate.

Read Aloud

Epic poetry is meant to be heard. Milton’s blank verse, Homer’s dactylic hexameter, Dante’s terza rima—the rhythm transforms the experience.

Not Exhaustive

Harold Bloom’s The Western Canon expands further into hundreds of titles. Non-Western parallels exist (Mahabharata, Bhagavad Gita, Gilgamesh), but this list stays closest to the Judeo-Christian epic and allegorical tradition.
